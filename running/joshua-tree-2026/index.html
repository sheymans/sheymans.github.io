Km 9–13, the climb. About half of the race's climbing came in these five km. Km 10 (11:58) was the only km slower than cutoff pace, and km 12 took 11:06. Cadence and heart rate dropped on both: those were the walk breaks on the steep grades, the race plan from the rehearsals.

What got it there. 24 weeks and 467.7 km, through an Achilles flare in July (a deload week and a week fully off), two rest weeks and a frozen right shoulder. Easy running only, with no speed work. The right-foot protocol ran three days a week throughout, and the Achilles was pain-free on every long run from the 16k on. Three dusk rehearsals (16k, 18k, 14k) set up the race routine, and the vest-strap fix after the 16k ended the mid-back pain.

BULLETPROOF THE RIGHT FOOT

Mid-portion Achilles · posterior tibialis · plantar fascia · the FOOT block on Mon/Wed/Fri in the log above
How to read it. 3×10 = 3 sets of 10 reps. each = one leg at a time (per foot). Tempo like 3-2-3 = seconds up · hold at top · down — moving that slowly is the exercise. Default tempos: A 3-2-3, C 2-1-3. E is a hold like ISO, but with the knees bent. load = backpack or dumbbells. ISO = isometric hold (see below). Rest 60–90s between sets. The whole point of this layout: heavy eccentric work only on your two easy-run days; Friday after the long run is light holds only — never stack heavy calf work on long-run legs.

The exercises — exactly how

ISO · Isometric calf hold Pain relief + tendon stiffness

Same setup as A (ToePro, toes elevated). Rise to about halfway up and hold dead still at that height for the full count — no bouncing, no lowering. Both legs early on; one leg once it's easy. Push hard enough that it's a real effort but pain stays ≤3/10. 4–5 × 30–45s, rest ~60s. These calm an irritable tendon and keep it primed without fatigue — that's why they're the only thing on long-run Fridays and in race week.

A · Standing single-leg heel raise, toes elevated Achilles (mid) + plantar fascia

On the ToePro with the balls of your feet on the ramp so your toes sit higher than your heels (this pre-tensions the plantar fascia). Hold a wall/rail for balance only. Rise straight up onto the ball of the foot (3s up), pause at the top (2s), then lower slowly (3s down) until the heel drops below the platform into a gentle stretch — full range is fine for your mid-portion Achilles. Cue: rise straight up over the big toe, don't let the ankle roll outward (this protects the posterior tibialis). Progress: both legs → single leg → add a loaded backpack/dumbbell.

B · Seated heel raise (retired after wk 15) Soleus — replaced by E

Sit, balls of feet on the ToePro/step edge, weight resting on the knees, knees bent ~90°. Push up (2–3s), pause (1–2s), lower slowly (3s) to a full stretch. Dropped from wk 16 because it meant breaking down the standing ToePro setup — E below does the same job standing. Kept here so the early weeks in the log still read correctly.

E · Bent-knee calf hold (standing) Soleus — the downhill protector

Your normal ToePro setup, nothing to move. Balls of the feet on the ramp, hand on a wall for balance, and bend the knees to about 30° — a shallow quarter-squat — holding that knee bend the whole time. Rise to about halfway up and hold dead still for the count.

Why the bent knee is the whole point. The gastrocnemius crosses the knee, so bending it goes slack and hands the work to the soleus underneath — the biggest plantarflexor in running, the one that fatigues over 21k and the one that protects the Achilles on downhills and in loose sand. Straight-knee work (A, and the standing ISO) does not reach it, which is why this earns its own slot.

Both legs first, single leg once that is easy. Same pain rule as everything else. Prefer reps? Same position, 3×12 slow bent-knee heel raises does the same job — the knee angle is what matters, holds versus reps does not.

C · Resisted inversion Posterior tibialis (below the inner ankle)

Sit with the leg out, loop a resistance band around the forefoot, anchored so it pulls your foot outward. Keep the heel still and turn the sole inward and down against the band (2s), then control back (3s). No band? Do single-leg heel raises with a rolled towel or small ball squeezed between your inner ankles — it forces you up over the big toe and biases the posterior tibialis.

D · Pogo hops (from wk 16) Elastic / spring capacity

Small, fast hops in place off the balls of your feet — stiff ankles, minimal knee bend, quick ground contact, like skipping rope. Double-leg 3×20 → progress to single-leg sets of 10–15. This builds the tendon's spring so it tolerates downhills and Joshua Tree's loose sand — the two things that make an Achilles work hardest.

Pre-run ritual — ~7 min, before every run

1 · ISO primer. 2×40s calf holds (both legs, ToePro or a step) right before heading out. Loading dampens tendon pain for hours — you're pre-medicating with load.
2 · Walk 5 minutes briskly before any running. Non-negotiable — the tendon is stiffest in the first minutes after waking, and that's exactly when the flare hit.
3 · First km flat & deliberately slow. Route-plan it: no inclines and no descents in the first 10 minutes. Hills only once warm — uphills before downhills.
4 · Shoes. Moderate-to-high heel drop (8–12 mm), retire pairs past ~600 km — a compressed midsole quietly raises Achilles strain. Small heel-lift inserts (both shoes) during twitchy periods.
5 · No calf stretching before runs. Stretching pulls on sensitized tissue; loading calms it. The holds are the warm-up.
Daily check. Rate first steps out of bed — calm / stiff / painful. It drifts upward days before a flare announces itself; a drift means hold the next progression, not stop.
Pain rule. Up to 5/10 during the exercise is OK if it's back to normal by the next morning and isn't getting worse week to week. If morning pain climbs, you limp, or it sharpens — drop back one level (less weight / both legs / fewer reps). Don't push through.
General training info, not medical advice. These symptoms are recurring — if pain worsens or doesn't settle, get a hands-on assessment from a physio or sports-medicine clinician.
