WHAT PACE IS MINE?

Two tests set the goal: a 5k on Wed Dec 16 (wk 6) and a 10k on Wed Jan 20 (wk 11). The 10k counts more.
How it converts. MP = 10k pace × 1.13, or 5k pace × 1.20. That is a deliberately cautious conversion for three runs a week; the textbook ratios assume more mileage than this. Run the tests flat and evenly paced; a sprint finish doesn't count.

What to do with it. 15+ min inside the cutoff: race at MP and start with the pace team at or just under your finish. 5–15 min: run the first half 5 s/km slower than MP and walk only at aid stations. Under 5 min, or outside: tell me. Wednesdays get more MP work, the race becomes a run/walk, and the 6:30 pace team becomes the wall you stay ahead of.

The exercises — exactly how

ISO · Isometric calf hold Pain relief + tendon stiffness

ToePro, toes elevated. Rise about halfway up and hold dead still for the full count: no bouncing, no lowering. A real effort, pain ≤3/10. These calm an irritable tendon and prime it without fatigue. That's why they're the only foot work on long-run days and in race week.

A · Single-leg heel raise, toes elevated, loaded Achilles (mid-portion) + plantar fascia

Balls of the feet on the ToePro so the toes sit higher than the heels. Hold a wall for balance only, with the dumbbell in the hand on the working side. Rise (3 s), pause (2 s), lower on one leg (3 s) until the heel drops below the platform. Cue: rise straight up over the big toe and don't let the ankle roll outward (this protects the posterior tibialis). If the load makes the single-leg rise ugly, rise on two and lower on one. The slow single-leg lower is the part that counts.

E · Bent-knee calf hold Soleus · weeks 2–8

Same setup, knees bent ~30° the whole time, rise halfway and hold. The bent knee slackens the gastrocnemius and hands the work to the soleus: the muscle that fatigues late in a long race and protects the Achilles on descents. From wk 5, hold a 15 lb dumbbell.

S · Seated soleus raise, barbell (from wk 9) Soleus, heavy — replaces E

Sit on the bench, balls of the feet on the ToePro or a plate, knees at 90°. Lay the barbell across the thighs just above the knees, on a folded towel. Push up (2 s), hold (1 s), lower into the stretch (3 s). Start with the empty bar, 3×10. Add 5 kg when the last set feels like 7/10. The soleus takes several times your body weight on every stride, and this is the only exercise in the plan that loads it anywhere near that. That's why it arrives as the long runs pass 20k. Shoulder: roll the bar up from the floor onto your thighs. It never needs to go near the rack position.

C · Resisted inversion Posterior tibialis — below the inner ankle

Sit with the leg out and loop the band around the forefoot, anchored so it pulls the foot outward. Keep the heel still and turn the sole inward and down (2 s), then come back with control (3 s). Late in a marathon, a tired posterior tibialis lets the arch drop. That's what this prevents.

D · Pogos → hop-and-stick Elastic capacity — the tendon as a spring

Pogos: small, fast hops off the balls of the feet. Stiff ankles, minimal knee bend, quick contacts, like skipping rope. Double-leg first, then single-leg. Hop-and-stick (from wk 8): hop forward ~30–50 cm on one leg and land, then freeze for 2 s without the knee or arch caving in. This trains the landing, and 42 km is about 35,000 landings.

Pre-run ritual — ~7 min, before every run

1 · ISO primer. 2×40s calf holds right before heading out. Load dampens tendon pain for hours.
2 · Walk 5 minutes briskly before any running, outside in the morning light (it doubles as the light protocol below).
3 · First km flat and deliberately slow. Hills only once warm, uphills before downhills.
4 · Shoes. 8–12 mm drop, retired past ~600 km. This block is ~580 km on its own: start it in a fresh pair. Buy race shoes by mid-January, so they've done the wk-10 rehearsal and at least one more long run before Mar 7.
5 · No calf stretching before runs. The holds are the warm-up.
Daily check. Rate the first steps out of bed: calm / stiff / painful. A drift upward means hold the next progression, not stop.
Pain rule. Up to 5/10 during the exercise or run is OK if it's back to normal by the next morning and isn't getting worse week to week. If morning pain climbs, you limp, or it sharpens, drop back one level (less weight / fewer sets / shorter run). Don't push through. A marathon block forgives a lost week; it doesn't forgive a lost month.

TUE & THU — LEGS, NOT SHOULDER

Bench · pull-up bar · barbell · 2 dumbbells (+ kettlebell, bands) · ~40 min Tue, ~30 min Thu · starts Tue Nov 10
Why lift for a marathon. Heavy, slow strength work improves running economy and protects against the overuse injuries that end marathon blocks. Stronger hips and hamstrings hold the stride together at 35k, and strong quads take the descents.

The shoulder rule. In every exercise here, the right arm either hangs at your side or stays out of the work. Anything that needs the arm overhead, behind the head or rotated outward is parked until the shoulder earns it back: back squat, front squat, overhead press, bench press, pull-ups. See the ladder at the bottom.

Tuesday is the heavy day: the day after an easy run, three days before the long run. Thursday is light: it's the day before the long run, so nothing to failure and nothing that leaves you sore. RPE = effort out of 10; RPE 7 means about three good reps left.

Phases: Foundation wk 1–7: learn the moves, 3×8, RPE 6–7 · Strength wk 8–12: Tuesday goes heavier, 4×5, RPE 8 · Maintain wk 13–16: same weights, fewer sets · Race week: activation only. Test weeks (6 and 11) take Tuesday light.

Tuesday — Legs A heavy · ~40 min

Warm-up · 5 min

10 glute bridges · 10 bodyweight good-mornings · 5 split squats each side · 10 leg swings each way.

1 · Romanian deadlift, barbell Hamstrings · glutes · back3×8 → 4×5 → 3×5

Stand tall with the bar at your thighs, double overhand grip at shoulder width. Soften the knees, push the hips back and slide the bar down the thighs to just below the knees, back flat (3 s down). Then drive the hips through to stand. Shoulder: the arms just hang. That's traction, not range, and a frozen shoulder usually tolerates it. If the right one objects, use two dumbbells at your sides, palms in.

2 · Bulgarian split squat Quads · glutes · single-leg control3×8 each → 4×6 each → 3×6

Back foot laces-down on the bench, dumbbells hanging at your sides. Lower until the back knee nearly touches the floor (3 s), then drive up through the front heel. It builds quad strength at long muscle length, which is what the downhill miles ask for. Start at bodyweight and add dumbbells once 3×8 is easy.

3 · Hip thrust Glutes3×10 → 4×8 → 3×8

Upper back across the side of the bench, feet flat. Load goes across the hip crease on a folded towel: a dumbbell at first, the barbell once one dumbbell isn't enough. Drive the hips up until knees, hips and shoulders line up, pause 2 s, lower. Shoulder: steady the weight with the left hand and let the right arm rest on the bench.

4 · Suitcase carry Core (anti-lean) · grip3×30–40 m each side

Dumbbell or kettlebell in one hand. Walk tall without leaning toward it. Your right arm held the kettlebell pain-free in September: holding with the arm at your side is one of the safest loads a frozen shoulder can take. If it aches, carry on the left only.

5 · Dead bug, legs only Deep core3×8 each

On your back, hips and knees at 90°, arms crossed on the chest so the right arm stays out of it. Lower one heel to tap the floor while the low back stays pressed down, return, switch. Slow.

6 · Left arm · prone row Cross-education3×10 → 3×8

Lie face down along the bench and row a dumbbell to your hip with the left arm. The right arm hangs off the bench like a free pendulum. Training one arm measurably slows strength loss in the other (the cross-education effect), so the left side is doing the right a favour while it waits.

7 · Right arm · isometrics Keeps the cuff switched on5×10 s × 3 directions

Stand in a doorframe, right elbow tucked at your side and bent 90°. Press the back of the wrist outward into the frame at about 25% effort, with nothing moving. Then press the palm inward, then the outer arm outward against the wall. Skip any direction that hurts and try it again in two weeks.

Thursday — Legs B light & springy · ~30 min · RPE ≤6

1 · Step-up onto the bench Quads · glutes3×8 each → 2×8

Dumbbells at your sides, whole foot on the bench. Drive up through that heel without pushing off the back foot, then step down slowly (2 s). Keep it light: tomorrow is the long run.

2 · Single-leg RDL Hamstring · balance · ankle stabilisers3×8 each

One dumbbell in the hand opposite the standing leg. Hinge on one leg with the back leg reaching behind and the hips square. If the right hand can't hold it comfortably, do that side at bodyweight.

3 · Tibialis raise Shins — for the descents2×20 → 3×20

Back against a wall, heels ~30 cm out. Lift the toes toward the shins, then lower slowly. LA opens with a steep drop in the first mile.

4 · Side-lying hip abduction Glute medius2×15 each (or band walks 2×10 steps each way)

Top leg slightly behind you, toes forward, lift from the side of the hip. A tired glute med lets the knee and arch collapse inward late in the race, and that is exactly the load that irritates the posterior tibialis.

5 · Single-leg balance, eyes closed Ankle proprioception3×30 s each

Barefoot. Much of the fine control comes from the posterior tibialis, so this is foot rehab disguised as balance.

6 · McGill curl-up + left side plank Trunk3×8 (8 s holds) · 3×30 s

Curl-up: on your back, one knee bent, hands under the small of the back (or the right arm on your stomach). Lift head and shoulders a few cm as one piece and hold 8 s. Side plank on the left forearm only. The right side waits for Phase B, because it puts body weight through the right shoulder.

7 · Left arm · floor press + right isometrics Cross-education3×10 · as Tuesday

On the floor, press a dumbbell up with the left arm. The floor stops the elbow, which is why this is also the first right-arm press to come back later.

Bringing the right arm back by gate, not by date

Phase A · irritable — now The arm rests in the gym

Isometrics only. Carries at your side and hanging-arm lifts (RDL) as long as they're pain-free.

Phase B · settling Gate: sleeping through, no flare past 24 h, morning stiffness only minutes

Add band external/internal rotation with the elbow at the side, 3×15. Add band rows with the elbows at the sides, 3×15, and a light right-arm floor press, 3×10. Short side planks on the right forearm. Pallof press.

Phase C · thawing Gate: passive ER ≥ ~65° (about ¾ of the left)

Two-arm prone rows · push-ups, hands on the bench first, then the floor · dumbbell bench press · goblet squat · supported hang from the pull-up bar with the feet on the floor, letting body weight open the overhead range.

Phase D · nearly symmetric — likely after the marathon Gate: ER within ~10° of the left, full overhead reach

Full dead hangs → band-assisted pull-ups → pull-ups · overhead press · barbell back squat once the hands can reach the bar comfortably.

THE RIGHT SHOULDER — EVERY DAY

Frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis) · onset late Aug 2026 · 2 × ~6 min a day · starts today
What it is. The joint capsule has thickened and shrunk. On Sep 12, passive external rotation stopped hard at ~45° on the right against 90° on the left. A capsule left alone keeps shrinking, and one forced while it's irritable flares. So the treatment is gentle range, little and often, every day.

The rule. Move to the first stretch, never into pain. Nothing above ~3–4/10, settled within 30 minutes, and the next morning no worse. A flare that lasts more than a day means you went too far: drop back a level, don't stop.

When. Right after the morning shower (warm tissue moves further) and again in the evening. Anchor it to things you already do every day. Six minutes twice beats twenty minutes once.

The routine — in this order

1 · Pendulum Unloading · pain relief1 min

Bend forward with the left hand on the bench and let the right arm hang completely dead. Use the body, not the arm, to make small circles, 20 each way, then gentle swings front-to-back and side-to-side. If the shoulder muscles switch on, you're doing it wrong.

2 · Stick-assisted external rotation The priority — the range you've lost most5 × 20–30 s

On your back, rolled towel under the right elbow, elbow tucked and bent 90°. Hold a broomstick in both hands. The left arm does the pushing and rotates the right forearm outward toward the floor. Go to the first stretch, hold, release. If you only do one thing, do this one.

3 · Stick-assisted forward elevation Keep what you still have5 × 15 s

Still on your back, stick in both hands with the arms straight. The left arm lifts both arms up and overhead as far as is comfortable. Holding range is far easier than winning it back.

4 · Cross-body stretch Back of the capsule3 × 20–30 s

Right arm across the chest. The left hand cups the upper arm above the elbow and pulls gently. Keep the right shoulder down, not shrugged.

5 · Towel behind the back Internal rotation3 × 20 s

Towel over the left shoulder, hanging down your back. The right hand holds the bottom end behind you, and the left hand pulls gently upward, walking the right hand up the spine. Easy to overdo, so go gently.

6 · Shoulder-blade setting Keeps the scapula working while the joint can't10 × 5 s

Sit or stand tall and draw both shoulder blades gently down and back. Hold, release. The arm doesn't move. Also do it at your desk whenever you notice the right shoulder creeping up toward your ear.

Progressing same gates as the gym ladder

Phase B · settling

Holds go to 30–60 s, into mild discomfort rather than stopping at the first stretch. Add a doorway external-rotation stretch (forearm on the frame, turn the body away) and table slides (seated, slide the hand forward along a table and lean in).

Phase C · thawing

End-range holds, the supported hang from the pull-up bar, and the gym ladder's right-arm strength work. The thaw is where range comes back fastest. Keep the daily habit, because it's what makes the thaw complete.

Re-test log every other Sunday evening

Same setup as September. On your back, towel under the elbow, elbow bent 90° at your side, fully relaxed, with the left hand doing the work. Photograph from the feet. Always in the evening, because mornings are stiffest.

TARGET WEIGHT — 80 KG

1.845 m · 86.5 kg today (BMI 25.4) → 80 kg by Feb 21 (BMI 23.5) · waist under 92 cm
Why 80. It's a race weight you can train at, not a crash number: a BMI of 23.5, mid-healthy range and lean for a 46-year-old who lifts. Every kilo you don't carry is a kilo you don't lift ~35,000 times. The old rule of thumb, ~2 seconds per mile per pound, puts 6.5 kg at roughly 15–20 s/km. Treat that as a rough guide, but on a day when the cutoff matters, it's on the order of 10 minutes.

Why not lower. Below ~78 kg at your height, on 40+ km weeks, you start losing muscle and getting ill more often, and tendons heal on food. The better health number is the waist: under 92 cm (half your height). Measure at the navel on the first Sunday of each month.

The rate. ~0.35 kg a week: about 400 kcal a day under maintenance (~2,900, estimated). It comes from rest and gym days and from evenings, never from long-run days.

Protein every day

  • 160 g, as four feedings of ~40 g: eggs, Greek yoghurt, fish, chicken, beans and lentils, whey.
  • It protects muscle in a deficit and feeds tendon remodelling.

Carbs follow the training

  • Most on Friday and Thursday dinner, least on rest days.
  • Never diet the long run: eat before, fuel during (60 g/h), and eat a recovery meal within an hour (~80 g carbs + 40 g protein).

Where the 400 kcal comes from

  • Alcohol, evening snacking, liquid calories, ultra-processed snacks.
  • Not breakfast, not long-run days.
  • 30+ g fibre, vegetables at two meals, oily fish twice a week.

Steer by the scale

  • Weigh Mon/Wed/Fri on waking, after the toilet, before food. Read only the weekly average.
  • Losing <0.2 kg/wk for two weeks → trim ~150 kcal, in the evening.
  • Losing >0.6 kg/wk, or long runs feel flat → add ~200 kcal of carbs.

THE DAY — HUBERMAN-STYLE

Light, sleep, caffeine, food timing, cold and heat, fitted around the training. Times assume a 6:00 wake and morning sessions. If your day runs on a different clock, shift the times and keep the gaps.

Mon · Wed run day

  • ISO primer, 5-minute walk in the morning light, run, FOOT block.
  • Eat within the hour. Carbs ~320 g.
  • Cold shower ending, 1–3 min.

Tue · Thu lift day

  • Lift at least 3 h before bed. No cold within ~6 h after lifting: it blunts the strength signal.
  • 40 g protein after. Carbs ~230 g, more at Thursday dinner.
  • 8–10k steps.

Fri long run

  • Breakfast 2.5–3 h before. From wk 10, rehearse 4:00 for a 7:00 start.
  • Caffeine ~3 mg/kg (~250 mg) 45–60 min before: the one day it's early, on purpose.
  • Fuel 60 g/h. Weigh before and after (1 kg lost ≈ 1 L of sweat). Recovery meal within the hour.
  • NSDR and legs up in the afternoon. The biggest eating day of the week.

Sat · Sun recovery

  • A 45–90 min walk or easy hike, not a run.
  • Sauna or a hot bath, then the long version of the shoulder routine while you're warm.
  • Plan and prep the week's food. Same wake time.
  • Every other Sunday evening: shoulder re-test.

The weekly protocols

Cold · ~11 min a week

As 1–3 min cold endings to the shower on Mon/Wed/Sat/Sun, finishing on cold. Never within ~6 h after lifting. Do the shoulder routine before the cold: cold stiffens the capsule, heat loosens it.

Heat · ~57 min a week

Sauna, about 3 × 20 min at 80–90 °C, or hot baths if that's what you have. It expands plasma volume, and from February it's heat acclimation for an LA morning that can turn warm. Do the shoulder routine straight after, when the capsule is at its loosest. Not the evening before a long run, and drink a litre after.

Alcohol · none during the build

The single change that improves sleep, weight and recovery at once. If you do drink, no more than 2 a week, never within 3 h of bed or the night before a long run.

Breathing

The physiological sigh: two inhales through the nose, one long exhale through the mouth. Do 1–3 rounds whenever you're stressed, and on the start line. On easy runs: if you can't breathe through your nose, slow down.

Supplements · check with your doctor first

Creatine monohydrate 5 g/day for strength and recovery. It adds ~1 kg of water in the first weeks, so if you take it, start now so that lands before the weight trend matters. Omega-3, ≥1 g EPA a day. Vitamin D3, dosed to a blood level. Magnesium (bisglycinate or threonate) before bed, optional. Optional for the tendon: 15 g gelatine or collagen with vitamin C ~45 min before the Monday foot session; there's early evidence it boosts collagen synthesis. At your next blood draw, ask for ferritin and 25-OH vitamin D: runners doing 40+ km a week drift low on iron.

Pacing

  • The first mile drops steeply. Brake and save the quads.
  • First 10k at MP + 10 s/km. The course rolls all the way, so don't bank time in the first six miles.
  • Hold MP to 30k, then use what's left.
  • Pace teams run 3:00 → 6:30 in 10-minute steps. Start with the one that matches your test, and stay well ahead of 6:30.

Fuel & drink

  • One 30 g gel every 30 min from minute 30 (~60 g/h, about 11 gels over six hours).
  • The course's gels at miles 12 and 18 are top-ups. Carry the rest.
  • Water and Electrolit every ~1.5 miles: walk 15–20 s, drink, go. That's the rhythm you rehearsed.

If the Achilles talks

  • Switch to run 4 min / walk 1 min and finish it that way.
  • The pain rule doesn't change on race day.
  • After: keep walking, eat within the hour, and walk only for the next week.
